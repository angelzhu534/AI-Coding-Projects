Financial Contingency
Playbook

The rules we agree to now, while it's calm, so that if cash gets tight in the first six months we're reading numbers against pre-agreed lines instead of deciding under pressure.

Prepared byAngel Zhu
StatusDraft — numbers to confirm
HorizonFirst 6 months of operations

What this document is for

A cash problem and a failing business look identical on a bank statement. This playbook exists to tell them apart — and to decide, in advance, what we do about each. It turns the founder's contingency plan into something with numbers attached, so the hardest call of the startup phase is mostly made before we ever face it.

The idea

Why decide any of this now?

The quality of a decision made calmly, in advance, against defined criteria is high. The quality of the same decision made under pressure — cash draining, emotions up, the founder's own money on the line — is low. That's true for everyone, however capable.

So the way we make a good decision in month four is to make most of it now. This playbook is that work. When a cash-danger moment arrives, we shouldn't be debating what "healthy" means or whether to keep going — we should be looking at the dashboard, comparing to lines we already drew, and executing a response we already agreed to.

The goal isn't to be wise in the crisis. It's to make sure that by the time the crisis comes, the decision has already been made.

Structure

The five layers

A contingency system isn't just a list of warning signs. It's five connected layers, and skipping any of them is why most contingency plans fail when they're actually needed.

LayerWhat it answersWhere it lives
1 · MetricsWhat we measure, defined precisely enough that two people agree on the numberSection: The three vital signs
2 · ThresholdsThe lines that mean something — target, warning, breachSection: Where the lines are

The most common mistake

Most plans build layer 3 — a list of "if this bad thing happens" — and skip the rest. Without precise metrics (layer 1) two people argue about whether the number is even bad. Without a warning line short of the breach line (layer 2) there's no time to react. And without governance (layer 5) the plan gets ignored the moment someone disagrees with it. All five or it doesn't hold.

Layer 1 — Metrics

The three vital signs

Three things can independently sink this business. Each has a different failure signature and a different fix, so each is tracked separately.

Volume — can we get the calls?

The master metric. If this fails, nothing else matters. But the level alone isn't enough — a business at 1.1 calls/day and climbing is healthy; at 1.4 and flat is in trouble. So volume is tracked as two numbers: level and trajectory.

  • Definition: trailing 28-day average of completed, billed calls ÷ operating days.
  • Trajectory: week-over-week change in that average, over a rolling 4-week window.
  • Source: dispatch log / job record. Cadence: weekly.

Price — is each call profitable?

This is the pricing-power risk: as a new entrant we may have to undersell to win accounts. The subtlety is mix — if we start winning cheaper call types and losing premium ones, average revenue-per-call falls even if we never discounted a single job. So we watch revenue-per-call and, where possible, split it by call type so we can tell "we're underpricing" from "our mix shifted."

  • Definition: total billed revenue ÷ completed calls, monthly. Ideally also per call type.
  • Reference: $530.63/call blended, $383.41 gross profit/call in the model.
  • Source: invoicing. Cadence: monthly.

Runway — do we run out before either resolves?

This is the trigger for the whole plan, not a viability question itself. The level ($25K floor) matters, but so does the rate — $30K holding flat is fine; $30K falling $8K/month is a countdown. Burn coverage turns a cash level into that countdown.

  • Definition: closing cash balance, and burn coverage = cash ÷ average monthly recurring outflow.
  • Floor: $25,000 (roughly one month of running costs).
  • Source: bank + the cash flow model. Cadence: weekly.

Layer 2 — Thresholds

Where the lines are

These come from the model — they're not round numbers, they're the points where the business actually changes behavior. Every threshold is time-indexed, because a given call rate means something different in month 2 (ramp) than in month 5 (mature). Applying a mature-state line to a ramping business is how you either panic early or miss a real problem late.

What this means

The single van holds up all the way down to 1.25 calls/day — 38% below target — before cash goes negative. That's a huge margin of safety, and it's the strongest argument for the conservative approach: not over-borrowing, keeping the operation lean, letting the business prove demand before we add capacity.

It also tells us the warning line worth watching is 1.38 calls/day sustained. Above it, we're fine. Between 1.25 and 1.38, we're eating into the floor and the contingency plan is live. Below 1.25, we're in real trouble.

Price threshold

Holding volume at plan, the model shows revenue-per-call can fall meaningfully before the floor is breached — but this is the metric to watch most closely, because a discount is permanent in a way a slow month isn't. The founder should set the hard floor here; the model gives the outer bound, but the commercial judgment of "the lowest rate we'll accept to win an account" is his call.

Layer 3 & 4 — Triggers and responses

The decision tree

Cash falling below the $25K floor inside the first six months starts the process. From there, the response depends on what the vital signs say — and critically, it's usually the combination that tells the story, not any single metric.

A
Diagnose — what do the vital signs say?

Cash hit the floor. Before any money moves, read the three vital signs against their lines. Is volume climbing or flat? Is revenue-per-call holding or eroding? Is this a runway problem or a viability problem?

B
Decide — do the economics justify more money?

The core judgment. If unit economics are sound and volume is trending up, the shortfall is a runway problem, not a failure — safe to add capital. This is Angel's call, made against the pre-agreed lines above. Green light only if: contribution margin within range of model and volume trajectory positive over trailing 4 weeks.

C
Fund — founder bridge loan

If B is green: board vote → corporate resolution → founder makes a bridge loan on the same terms as the existing personal loan (deferred, rate-capped, paused interest capitalization). Sized per the bridge analysis in the next section.

E
If it's a time problem — bridge only, reassess in 1 month

Van isn't full yet, we just need time to keep filling it or win better-priced work. Deploy the bridge, hold, reassess in one month. Still unhealthy? Escalate to F.

F
If it's a capacity problem — scale

Van is already full but revenue still isn't enough. That's a scale signal: take the Banner equipment loan for a second van (the van is collateral) and cut OpEx hard. This is the only branch that adds fixed cost, so it requires the demand to be genuinely proven first.

D
If the economics are broken — cut hard or wind down

If B is red — volume flat and below breach, revenue-per-call below floor — no bridge. Execute deep cost cuts or an orderly wind-down. This is the branch everyone avoids emotionally, which is exactly why its trigger is defined in advance.

The kill line — define this now

D is the mirror of B, and it needs a pre-agreed trigger just as much. Suggested, to confirm: if by month 4 calls/day is below 1.25 and not growing over the trailing 4 weeks, and revenue-per-call is below the founder's floor, we execute D. Naming this now — while it's abstract — is what stops the business from dying slowly by bridge loan, one justified-in-the-moment decision at a time.

Layer 4 — The bridge loan

What a bridge actually buys

The bridge is a bet that time fixes the problem. The modeling question is precise: how much runway does a bridge of size X buy, and does its structure help or hurt the trough?

The deferral is what makes it work

Because the bridge matches the personal loan's deferred structure, it adds cash without adding a monthly payment during the danger window. That's the whole point — an amortizing loan would deepen the very trough it's meant to fix.

Same trough (the deferral means no payments hit during the window either way at this horizon), but the deferred version leaves ~$20K more at month 24 because less interest is paid inside the window.

Sizing — a menu, not a guess

At 75% revenue — a real underperformance scenario — the single van already survives without a bridge (trough $46,338). So the bridge is insurance for the worse cases, or a cushion to remove the danger months entirely. Here's what each size does, deployed at month 3:

The honest read on the bridge

At 75% revenue the business doesn't actually need a bridge to survive — that's the resilience showing again. The bridge matters in the worse scenarios (below ~1.3 calls/day sustained) and as a way to buy diagnosis time without panic.

The number that keeps the bridge honest isn't what it buys — it's what it risks. A bridge deployed into a business that doesn't recover just deepens the hole by its own size. That's why the bridge only fires after B is green: the diagnosis has to say "working but slow," or the bridge is throwing good money after bad.

Layer 5 — Governance

Who decides, and what if we disagree

This is the layer that makes the plan real rather than a document nobody follows. Two things need settling now, while they're hypothetical and collegial — because in month 4 they won't be either.

The impartial seat

The step-B call is handed to Angel deliberately, because the founder-lender is conflicted — every bias (sunk cost, optimism, ego) pushes a conflicted party toward funding one more month. That's not a character flaw, it's the predictable psychology of having your own money and your own thesis on the line.

But the impartiality has a limit worth naming: Angel holds equity too, so she also wants the business to live. Her discipline is a single question, applied to every fund/don't-fund call — and to herself:

Would someone with no stake in this business — not a founder, not an owner, a stranger with capital — put money in right now, on these exact numbers?

If the honest answer is no, funding it is attachment talking, not merit.

The disagreement rule — the gap in the current plan

The plan says Angel is the impartial advisor but never says what governs if Angel and the founder read step B differently. That's the exact scenario the seat exists for. It needs a decision, now:

  • Option 1: Angel's assessment is binding on the fund/don't-fund decision. Clean, but concentrates the call in one person.
  • Option 2: A pre-agreed tiebreaker — a third read, or a specific metric that breaks the tie (e.g. "if calls/day trajectory is negative over 4 weeks, don't fund, regardless").

Either works. What doesn't work is leaving it undefined, because then the founder's read simply overrides Angel's by default and the impartial seat is decorative.

Review cadence

  • Weekly: volume (level + trajectory) and cash. The fast-moving vital signs.
  • Monthly: revenue-per-call and cost variance against budget.
  • At any floor breach: the full A–F process convenes.

Before launch

What we need to lock down

  1. Confirm the three make/break numbers.

    Minimum calls/day by month 3 (model suggests 1.3+), the revenue-per-call floor (founder-set), and the cash floor ($25K). Everything keys off these.

  2. Set the kill line for D.

    The specific combination — volume below breach and not growing, price below floor — that triggers a wind-down or deep cuts. Define it while it's abstract.

  3. Settle the disagreement rule.

    Binding Angel call, or a defined tiebreaker. Pick one now.

  4. Confirm the expense-tracking tool.

    So variance tracking is live day one, not reconstructed later.

  5. Get the cuttable line-item list.

    From the founder — which OpEx comes out, in what order, if D or F requires cuts. Needed to model D properly.

  6. Define demand KPIs.

    Working with Ansley's research now, and with live data as it comes. The leading indicator that moves before call volume.
